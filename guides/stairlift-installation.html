Stairlift installation: costs, types, and how to choose an installer

A stairlift lets someone ride a powered seat along a rail instead of climbing the stairs. A straight-rail unit typically costs about $2,000 to $9,000 installed; curved stairs cost more because the rail is custom-made. The installer and the service agreement matter as much as the brand.

By the Aging Gracefully Editorial Team · Published November 6, 2026

This guide is educational and organizational. It is not medical advice. If balance, strength, or transfers are changing, an occupational therapist can help decide whether a stairlift, a platform lift, a first-floor bedroom, or another option fits best.

Types of stairlifts

  • Straight-rail: for a single straight flight with no turns or landings. Usually the least expensive and quickest to install; reconditioned units are often available.
  • Curved: for stairs with turns, landings, or a spiral. The rail is measured and built for that staircase, so it costs more, takes longer, and has little resale value.
  • Outdoor: weather-resistant units for porch or deck steps.
  • Standing or perch: for people who find it hard to bend their knees to sit.
  • Platform or vertical lifts: carry a wheelchair user without transferring to a seat. These are a different product category with different installation requirements.

What a stairlift costs

Angi's 2026 cost guide puts a straight stairlift at roughly $2,000 to $9,000. Price depends on rail length, curves and landings, weight capacity, powered swivel or folding-rail options, indoor or outdoor use, and the installer's warranty and service terms. Always compare written quotes on the same specifications.

Who installs stairlifts

Most installations are done by mobility-equipment dealers or a manufacturer's authorized installers. Some accessibility remodelers and Certified Aging-in-Place Specialists (CAPS) also install lifts or coordinate the project. Because a stairlift needs ongoing service, a local installer who can respond to breakdowns is usually worth more than a small price difference.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. Are you authorized by the manufacturer to install and service this model?
  2. Is the quote itemized — unit, rail, options, installation, removal, and taxes?
  3. What do the warranties cover, for how long, and who performs repairs?
  4. What is your typical response time for a breakdown, and is there an emergency fee?
  5. Can you provide references from recent installations on similar staircases?
  6. What happens if we move or no longer need the lift — is there a buy-back or removal option?

The FTC's home-improvement guidance also applies: be cautious of high-pressure sales, large up-front deposits, and verbal-only promises.

Help paying for a stairlift

  • Original Medicare generally does not cover stairlifts because they are treated as home modifications rather than durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer limited home-safety benefits; check the plan's Evidence of Coverage.
  • Medicaid home- and community-based services waivers may help eligible people in some states. See Medicaid HCBS information and ask your state Medicaid agency.
  • Veterans: the VA's HISA grant excludes stair glides. Veterans with qualifying service-connected disabilities may be eligible for VA disability housing grants (SAH and SHA).
  • Local programs: your Area Agency on Aging may know of local repair or modification funds. Use the Eldercare Locator or call 211.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a stairlift cost?

A straight-rail stairlift typically costs about $2,000 to $9,000 installed, according to Angi (2026). Curved stairs need a custom rail and usually cost substantially more. Outdoor units, extra landings, powered swivel seats, and folding rails add to the price. Get at least two written, itemized quotes that list the rail, installation, warranty, and service terms.

Who installs stairlifts?

Most stairlifts are installed by mobility-equipment dealers or the manufacturer's authorized installers. Some accessibility contractors and Certified Aging-in-Place Specialists (CAPS) also install them or coordinate the work. Ask whether the installer is authorized for the brand, who handles warranty service, and how quickly they respond to breakdowns.

Does Medicare pay for a stairlift?

Original Medicare generally does not cover stairlifts because they are treated as home modifications rather than durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer limited supplemental home-safety benefits; check the plan's current Evidence of Coverage before assuming a stairlift is included.

Can veterans get help paying for a stairlift?

The VA's HISA grant excludes stair glides, so it is not a route for a standard stairlift. Veterans with qualifying service-connected disabilities may be eligible for the VA's Specially Adapted Housing (SAH) or Special Home Adaptation (SHA) grants. Ask the VA Prosthetic and Sensory Aids Service or a VA housing grant agent about current options.

Should I rent or buy a stairlift?

Renting can make sense for a short recovery period; buying usually costs less over several years. Reconditioned units can lower the price for straight stairs. Compare the total cost over the time you expect to need it, including installation, removal, and service.

How long does stairlift installation take?

A straight-rail stairlift is often installed in a few hours because the rail attaches to the stair treads rather than the wall. Curved rails must be measured and manufactured first, which can add weeks before installation.
