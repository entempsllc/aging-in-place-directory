Aging in place services: a complete guide for 2026

Aging in place services help older adults stay in their own homes safely. They span five categories: home modifications, in-home care, home health care, transportation, and legal and financial planning. This guide covers what each does, what it costs, and how to find help in your city.

By the Aging Gracefully Editorial Team · Published October 12, 2026

How to use this guide: This is general education, not individualized medical, legal, financial, or safety advice. Confirm coverage, eligibility, and product choices with the relevant plan, agency, or qualified professional.

What aging in place services are

Aging in place services are professional and community services that help older adults remain in their own homes rather than moving to a facility. The phrase covers a broad range — from a handyman installing a grab bar to a home health nurse managing medications after a hospital discharge. Most families do not use all of these at once; they start with the most pressing need and add services as circumstances change.

According to the National Institute on Aging, planning ahead is key: the best time to make home modifications is before a crisis, not after a fall or a hospital stay. A home that is easy to navigate at 70 may become difficult at 80, and small changes made early cost less than emergency modifications made under pressure.

The five categories of aging in place services

1. Home modifications and accessibility equipment

Physical changes to the home that make it safer and easier to navigate. This is the category our directory focuses on. Common modifications include:

  • Grab bars: $200 to $350 per bar installed (Angi, 2026). Placed in bathrooms at toilets, showers, and tubs. See our grab bar installation guide.
  • Walk-in shower or tub-to-shower conversion: $1,500 to $15,000 (Angi, 2026). See our bathroom remodel guide.
  • Wheelchair ramps: $1,100 to $5,500 for a standard entry ramp (HomeAdvisor, 2026), or $55 to $275 per linear foot.
  • Stairlifts: $2,300 to $24,000 installed, with straight stairlifts at $2,000 to $9,000 and curved at $10,350 to $25,000 (Angi, 2026).
  • Doorway widening: $200 per door for swing-clear hinges (no construction), or $700 to $8,000 for structural widening (HomeAdvisor, 2026).
  • Comfort-height toilet: $350 to $900 installed (HomeAdvisor, 2026).

These jobs are done by handymen for small items, licensed contractors or CAPS specialists for larger projects, and stairlift dealers for stairlifts.

2. In-home care (personal care and daily living)

Non-medical help with daily activities: bathing, dressing, grooming, meal preparation, light housekeeping, laundry, and companionship. Provided by home care agencies or privately hired aides. Typical cost is $25 to $35 per hour, with variation by region and agency model. Some agencies require minimum visit lengths (typically 2 to 4 hours). See our daily living assistance guide for more detail.

Home care is usually private-pay. Long-term care insurance may cover it if the policy includes it. Medicaid HCBS waivers may cover personal care for eligible beneficiaries. Original Medicare does not cover ongoing custodial care.

3. Home health care (skilled, prescribed care)

Medical care delivered at home by a licensed nurse, physical therapist, occupational therapist, or home health aide. Used after a hospital discharge, a surgery, or when a chronic condition requires skilled management. May include wound care, medication management, therapy, and patient education. See our healthcare at home guide for more.

Home health care may be covered by Medicare or insurance when prescribed by a doctor and the patient meets program rules. A home health aide through a Medicare-certified agency is different from a private home care aide — the former requires a plan of care ordered by a physician.

4. Transportation

When driving is no longer safe, transportation services help with medical appointments, errands, and social activities. Options include volunteer driver programs through Area Agencies on Aging, paratransit services, ride-share programs for seniors, and public transit. Many are free or low-cost. See our senior transportation guide.

5. Legal, financial, and benefits planning

Estate planning, advance directives, power of attorney, Medicaid planning, and benefits counseling. An elder-law attorney can help structure finances to qualify for Medicaid long-term care if needed. A benefits counselor at your Area Agency on Aging can help you understand Medicare, Medicaid, and VA benefits. See our financial and legal guide.

How to choose the right services

  1. Start with a needs assessment. Walk through the home with the person who will live there. What is difficult? Where are the trip and fall risks? Is the bathroom safe? Is the entry accessible? An occupational therapist can help if the needs are complex.
  2. Prioritize the bathroom. The bathroom is the highest-risk room in the home — wet surfaces, transfers on and off the toilet and in and out of the tub, and tight spaces. Start with grab bars, then a handheld showerhead, then evaluate a tub-to-shower conversion.
  3. Check funding before hiring. Call your Area Agency on Aging (find yours at eldercare.acl.gov) and ask about home modification programs, minor repair programs, and Medicaid waivers. Check VA benefits if applicable.
  4. Get three written quotes. For any project over $500, get three itemized quotes. Compare scope, not just total price. Ask each contractor about permits, warranty, and who handles what.
  5. Verify credentials. For home modifications, check if the contractor is CAPS-certified at nahb.org. For in-home care, verify the agency is licensed and bonded in your state.

Funding help for aging in place

  • VA HISA grant: For eligible veterans, covers home modifications. Check VA eligibility.
  • Medicaid HCBS waivers: May cover home modifications and personal care for eligible beneficiaries. Learn about Medicaid HCBS.
  • Area Agency on Aging: Your local AAA may offer minor home repair, volunteer handyman, or grant programs. Find your AAA.
  • USDA Section 504: Loans and grants for very-low-income rural homeowners. Check USDA eligibility.
  • Medicare Advantage supplemental benefits: Some plans offer home-safety modifications as a supplemental benefit. Check your plan's current Evidence of Coverage.

Verify current eligibility and covered services directly with the program before starting work.

Frequently asked questions

What are aging in place services?

Aging in place services are professional services that help older adults remain in their own homes safely and independently. They include home modifications (grab bars, walk-in showers, ramps), in-home care (personal care, meal preparation, medication management), transportation, home health care, legal and financial planning, and community programs like senior centers and meal delivery.

How much do aging in place services cost?

Costs vary widely by service type. Home modifications like grab bars cost $200 to $350 per bar. A walk-in shower conversion costs $1,500 to $15,000. A wheelchair ramp costs $1,100 to $5,500. In-home care runs $25 to $35 per hour for a home care aide. A stairlift costs $2,300 to $24,000 installed. Get written quotes for each service.

Does Medicare pay for aging in place services?

Original Medicare may cover skilled home health care (nursing, therapy) when prescribed and when the patient meets program rules. It generally does not cover home modifications, ongoing custodial care, or handyman services. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer supplemental home-safety benefits. Medicaid HCBS waivers may cover modifications and personal care for eligible beneficiaries.

What is the difference between home care and home health care?

Home care (also called custodial or personal care) provides help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, meal preparation, and housekeeping. It is usually private-pay. Home health care is skilled, prescribed care from a licensed nurse or therapist and may be covered by Medicare or insurance when medically necessary.

How do I find aging in place services in my area?

Start with the Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov to find your local Area Agency on Aging. Search our city directories for aging-in-place contractors. For in-home care, ask your AAA for a list of licensed agencies. For home modifications, use the NAHB CAPS directory at nahb.org to find certified specialists.

What should I do first when planning to age in place?

Start with a home safety assessment. Walk through each room and note trip hazards, difficult steps, narrow doorways, and bathroom safety issues. Consider an occupational therapist assessment ($150 to $400) if mobility is changing. Prioritize the bathroom (falls, water, transfers) and the entry (stairs, thresholds). Then get quotes for the top three modifications.
